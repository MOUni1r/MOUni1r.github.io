01

Data foundation

The analysis combines data-quality cleaning, time and trip-level EDA, statistical testing, feature engineering, regression model comparison, and a separate generous-tipping classification task.

Data quality

22,699 rows and 18 columns were loaded. Missing values and duplicates were 0. Negative fare and duration values were corrected by clipping only those invalid values.

Fare estimation

Linear Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Gradient Boosting were compared using validation MAE. Gradient Boosting was selected.

Tipping classification

Random Forest was selected using cross-validated F1. The final test F1 was 0.561936 with ROC AUC 0.645463.

Production thinking

Route aggregates were learned from training data only, but production use would require historical or out-of-fold construction and prediction-time availability checks.

04

What the model says

The headline result is useful only when paired with the trade-offs and statistical context around it.

Key finding

Payment method was associated with average fare.

+$1.22

Mean fare was $13.4297 for credit-card payments and $12.2135 for cash, a difference of $1.2162. The 95% confidence interval was $0.8690 to $1.5634, with Welch p = 6.797 × 10⁻¹². This is an observational association, not a causal effect.

05

What I'd recommend

Each finding here points to a concrete next step, not just a metric to report.

01

Treat the $1.22 card-vs-cash fare gap as something to investigate operationally, such as route mix or tipping norms by payment type, not as grounds to price customers differently. The data is observational, not experimental.

02

Flag fare predictions in the highest-fare segment as low-confidence. The model's error grows sharply there, so route those cases to manual pricing review instead of full automation.

03

Use the generous-tipping classifier to prioritize driver-facing service nudges, such as surfacing a likely high-tip trip, rather than a customer-facing decision, given its moderate 56% F1.

04

Before production use, rebuild the route-level aggregate features from historical or out-of-fold data only, since the current version can leak information across the train/test boundary if reused as-is.

06

Limitations & next steps

A strong case study should make its boundaries visible.

01The dataset is educational and may not represent real NYC taxi behavior.
02Payment analysis is observational and does not establish causality.
03The fare model has materially larger errors in the highest-fare segment, so further segment-level investigation is needed.
